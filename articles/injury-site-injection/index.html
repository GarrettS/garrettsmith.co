DIY Injury Repair and the Case for Injecting BPC-157, Tβ4, and GHK-Cu Locally

2026 — All Articles

For peptides that act on the injured tissue itself, that's where the needle should go.

BPC-157, Tβ4, and GHK-Cu act locally, so the goal is to get as much of the peptide into the injured tissue as possible. But how can we know the best way to do that, when nobody has measured how much reaches the tissue by any route?

The conventional answer is subcutaneous injection, with animal studies cited in support. The reasoning follows: intraperitoneal (IP) systemic administration heals injuries far from the injection site in rats, a subcutaneous (SC) injection is systemic, therefore subcutaneous administration should reach the injury, and this should work in humans. Additionally, a lot of anecdotal evidence supports this. It's working for a lot of people. But is it optimal, or can we do better?

On closer examination, the reasoning has a few problems that explain why SC administration of these locally-acting peptides might be suboptimal. Systemic uptake of IP administration is faster than SC, and rats distribute the absorbed peptide quickly throughout the entire organism.

Worse, for poorly perfused tissue (e.g. human tendon), an unknown but small percentage of the administered dose can be expected to reach the injury. BPC-157 further hampers this with its minutes-long plasma half-life, and GHK-Cu encounters plasma copper-binding proteins before reaching its target.

Faster IP uptake in rats, combined with rapid circulation through a smaller organism and better relative perfusion, means more intact peptide can reach the target sooner, with more of its active lifetime spent at the target.

Increasing the systemic dose to compensate is not risk-free. BPC-157 and thymosin beta-4 promote angiogenesis and cell migration, and thymosin beta-4 is also anti-apoptotic, raising concern about tumor promotion. GHK-Cu increases copper exposure, and repeated local injection could also produce high local copper concentrations over weeks. Local delivery aims to increase exposure at the injury without increasing whole-body exposure to get there.

Reconstitution, injection, and dosing protocol is summarized below, and below it, those recommendations are explained.

Where to inject

Where injured muscle can be safely accessed, IM injection is the obvious choice.

For mid-tendon injuries, blind injection cannot reliably place the dose in the tendon itself. Placement immediately adjacent to it may instead reach the paratenon, fascia, loose connective tissue, or nearby soft tissue, depending on the anatomy and depth.

For injuries near the musculotendinous junction, IM injection into the adjoining injured muscle can place the dose close to the lesion.

A closer look at human subcutaneous injection

Human subcutaneous doses leave the site to enter circulation, dispersing through the organism, with a fraction reaching the injured tissue through its blood supply.

Humans are roughly two orders of magnitude heavier than rats, with liters rather than milliliters of blood and centimeter rather than millimeter delivery paths to sparsely vascularized tendons.

Injecting subcutaneously over the injury does not change the delivery problem. Subcutaneous fat is superficial to the deep fascia and tendons below it, and that fascia is a barrier rather than a conduit. The dose still enters subcutaneous capillaries and lymphatics before reaching the target through circulation. A very deep SC injection immediately superficial to the deep fascia might improve regional exposure somewhat compared with abdominal SC injection, but that has not been shown, and blind placement is inconsistent.

Local injection starts in the target tissue, before systemic dilution. How much that advantage matters depends on the peptide.

BPC-157

Rat efficacy and the 200 mcg dose

IP BPC-157 works in rats. [Staresinic, 2003] found accelerated healing of transected Achilles tendon, and [Krivic, 2006] found improved tendon-to-bone healing, both by intraperitoneal administration at 10 mcg/kg/day.

The common 250 mcg human dose has quantitative support even though the convention predates it. The body-surface-area (BSA)-scaled dose supports about 200 mcg/day. BPC-157's short-lived exposure and its upregulation of rapidly turning-over growth hormone receptors, make BID repeated pulses improve signaling over single daily dosing.

This article uses 200 mcg per dose, twice daily. More frequent dosing might better maintain the signaling effect, but that has not been tested.

Organism scale and targeting

Available peptide is recirculated through the rat much faster, and with better expected perfusion to tendon.

Rats circulate the equivalent of their blood volume about 3.6 times per minute at rest, compared with about once per minute in humans. Over BPC-157's roughly 15-minute rat plasma half-life, that is about 54 blood-volume equivalents circulated versus about 15 in humans while much of the peptide remains intact.

This does not mean the intended target receives an entire blood volume each time. Tendon receives far less blood flow than muscle, so a systemic dose reaches it through a much smaller vascular supply. This holds across rats and humans.

But human tendon, compared with rat tendon, is thicker, yet similarly sparsely supplied through longer intratendinous microvasculature. This increases vascular resistance, diffusion distance, and peptide spread through surrounding tissue, reducing concentration with distance. Thus, the tendon delivery problem is likely worse in humans.

Blood-volume turnover — 250 g rat:

Heart rate     ≈ 330 beats/min
Stroke volume  ≈ 0.175 mL/beat
Cardiac output ≈ 330 × 0.175 = 58 mL/min
Blood volume   ≈ 16 mL
Turnover       ≈ 3.6 per min

∴ 58 mL/min ÷ 16 mL ≈ 3.6 blood-volume turnovers/min

Blood-volume turnover — 70 kg human:

Heart rate     ≈ 70 beats/min
Stroke volume  ≈ 70 mL/beat
Cardiac output ≈ 70 × 70 = 4.9 L/min
Blood volume   ≈ 5 L
Turnover       ≈ 1.0 per min

∴ 4.9 L/min ÷ 5 L ≈ 1 blood-volume turnover/min

Systemic delivery races degradation. In humans it runs a longer path at a slower pace.

BPC-157's rat plasma half-life is 15 min (more on this below). The human half-life is unknown, and the beagle's was 5.27 minutes, so the human side of this comparison may be worse than it looks [He, 2022].

How long is it there?

BPC-157 disappears rapidly from plasma. Intravenous elimination half-life was 15.2 minutes in rats and 5.27 minutes in beagles, under 30 minutes across all routes and doses [He, 2022]. The pharmacokinetic doses were selected by body-surface-area scaling to represent the same human-equivalent dose of about 3.2 mcg/kg. After intramuscular injection, plasma peaked at 3 minutes, and intact peptide fell below the study's detection limit by 4 hours. The result shows rapid plasma disappearance in two species, not the human half-life, and not why the species differed. What the peptide sets off can last longer, since receptor upregulation and growth-factor expression take hours to days.

The peptide is degraded, not merely hidden from the assay. Radiolabeled BPC-157 was rapidly broken into smaller peptide fragments and amino acids, with six metabolites identified and radiolabel excreted in urine and bile. At 72 hours, 54% of the administered radiolabel remained in the carcass, though the study did not establish the chemical form of all of that residual label.

Plasma levels do not measure tendon exposure. They cannot show how much peptide reaches the tendon, the concentration it reaches there, or how quickly it distributes through the tissue.

What local delivery is meant to exploit

BPC-157 has a measured concentration-response in the target cell type. [Chang, 2014] found growth hormone receptor expression in tendon fibroblasts rising with concentration, and [Hsieh, 2017] found VEGFR2 upregulation. If receptor expression tracks concentration, the route that raises concentration at the tendon should raise expression there. What Chang's data cannot give is the exchange rate between a high brief exposure and a low sustained one, because he held exposure at 24 hours and varied concentration.

Intramuscular (IM) BPC-157 still reaches systemic circulation. Systemic bioavailability after IM injection was 14 to 19% in rats and 45 to 51% in beagles [He, 2022]. Human intramuscular bioavailability is unknown, and subcutaneous bioavailability was not measured in either species. Tracer distribution was wide, covering skin, intestine, lung, myocardium, skeletal muscle, liver, spleen, and fat, and it was highest in the kidney. Skeletal muscle held less than plasma at 1 hour.

Dose and frequency

The growth hormone receptors BPC-157 upregulates turn over fast. Growth hormone receptor is short-lived, on the order of an hour or two at the cell surface [van Kerkhof, 2002] (in hamster cells, not human tendon), so the pool is unlikely to persist unchanged between daily doses. If that holds in tendon, receptor density tracks the rate at which receptors are being made, and synthesis rises with concentration.

That argues for frequency over duration, so twice-daily dosing has a better rationale than daily across a 20- to 40-day dosing window. It is an inference from cell-line and culture data, not a measured property of human tendon. Chang's earliest measurement was after 24 hours of continuous exposure, so what a brief pulse does to receptor expression in vivo remains unknown.

Additional dose past some point should produce diminishing returns. Cells will only build so many receptors. Increasing the local dose also increases the amount available to leave the site and enter circulation, which works against the exposure advantage the local route is meant to create.

Cancer risk

BPC-157 promotes blood vessel growth, and systemic administration spreads that signal through the whole body before any of it reaches the injury. Its upregulation and activation of VEGFR2 could theoretically support neovascularization within hidden malignant lesions.

Repeated IM injection at one site focuses the scope more locally. At a lower dose, that means less systemic pro-angiogenic activity and thus less theoretical risk.

Thymosin beta-4 vs TB-500

This article discusses thymosin beta-4, the full 43-amino-acid protein. TB-500 is a seven-amino-acid fragment from the middle of it. Sometimes thymosin beta-4 (TB4) is sold as TB-500. As for TB-500, the fragment retains the central actin-binding sequence but lacks the rest of TB-4's actin-contacting structure and cannot produce Ac-SDKP, an anti-fibrotic fragment. For a tendon, where the failure is disorganized scar, that is a reason to prefer the full-length peptide.

The human trials and pharmacokinetics are for full-length TB4, not the seven-amino-acid fragment. The full sequence has better scientific support. It contains the sequence N-acetyl-seryl-aspartyl-lysyl-proline (Ac-SDKP), with demonstrated anti-fibrotic effects [Kumar, 2016] [Conte, 2016]. A certificate of analysis (COA) distinguishes them by molecular mass, 4963 g/mol for the parent and about 889 g/mol for the fragment.

That does not mean TB-500 fragment is ineffective. It has the 17–23 actin-binding region that has been shown to promote angiogenesis, wound healing, and cell migration for healing. The overwhelming anecdotal consensus is that TB-500 works for injury recovery.

[Biçer, 2026] is the only controlled tendon study of TB-500, finding improved Achilles biomechanics and histology after systemic dosing (in rats). But the paper identifies the product only as Peptide Sciences TB-500 (27-155-PS). Peptide Sciences sold both full-length Tβ4 and the shorter fragment, so it's unclear what he used.

Tβ4 systemic vs local and mechanism of action

Tβ4 binds G-actin. G-actin is the soluble form of actin that cells assemble into filaments to change shape and move. Tβ4's binding of G-actin promotes migration of repair and endothelial cells through injured tissue.

[Biçer, 2026] gave rats 60 mcg/kg/day intraperitoneally for four weeks after Achilles transection and Kessler repair. TB-500 increased maximum load to failure and improved histological scores compared with controls. Combination treatment with BPC-157 gave no additive benefit, possibly limited by the four-week time frame.

Local administration also works. [Xu, 2013] found that 1 mcg of Tβ4 in fibrin, placed into a transected rat medial collateral ligament, improved collagen organization and biomechanics compared with fibrin alone.

[Malinda, 1999] compared topical and intraperitoneal Tβ4 in a full-thickness rat skin-wound model, measuring local versus systemic administration. Five mcg applied locally just twice produced at least the same healing effect, with increased collagen deposition and angiogenesis, as 60 mcg injected intraperitoneally every other day for seven days, despite using only about 4% as much Tβ4 cumulatively, with significant effects evident by day 4.

These results showed that compared to systemic administration, topical application was slightly more effective, despite using about 4% as much Tβ4 cumulatively. This casts doubt on any injury-homing mechanism from systemic administration.

The risk

Thymosin beta-4 promotes cell migration, angiogenesis, and anti-apoptotic signaling, all of which can also support tumor growth and metastasis. Local injection offers a more precise approach to get more of it to the target tissue at a more conservative dose, with less systemic exposure.

GHK-Cu

GHK-Cu has an additional delivery problem. In addition to dilution and circulatory challenges, GHK-Cu faces the problem of binding proteins, and with systemic administration, GHK-Cu can bind to albumin and other things, before reaching the target.

GHK-Cu delivers copper, which collagen needs for cross-linking. Copper is the cofactor for lysyl oxidase, the enzyme that cross-links collagen and elastin into load-bearing fibrils.

GHK-Cu's pro-collagen effects have been shown in skin and wounds, with separate orthopedic evidence of improved ligament-graft mechanics. Its mechanisms promote angiogenesis and suppresses inflammatory signaling, all relevant to healing muscle, fascia, and tendon in humans [Pickart & Margolina, 2018]. The effects scale with concentration, which favors local delivery.

GHK-Cu injected into rat wound chambers increased collagen, total protein, DNA, and glycosaminoglycans in a concentration-dependent manner [Maquart, 1993].

An orthopedic study on rats found intra-articular GHK-Cu improved early graft stiffness and knee laxity after ACL reconstruction [Fu, 2015].

In mice, systemic GHK-Cu preserved muscle mass, increased fiber cross-sectional area, and improved grip strength [Deng, 2023].

Injecting GHK-Cu

GHK-Cu can sting when injected. Diluting it with bacteriostatic water or other peptides in the syringe can reduce the sting. At 3 mL reconstitution, the BPC-157, Tβ4, and GHK-Cu doses total about 15–19.5 units (0.15–0.20 mL), usually enough volume without additional bacteriostatic water.

Copper catalyzes peptide oxidation, so premix blends like GLOW may degrade faster. Combining in the syringe is a brief version of the same exposure. Draw the GHK-Cu last and inject promptly rather than preparing doses ahead.

Reconstitute the vials separately, then draw them together in the same syringe immediately before injection.

Each dose is estimated near the low end of the effective animal range, checked against acute and chronic animal exposures. Local animal doses are not converted by body surface area, because local concentration, target volume, and clearance determine exposure. A dose injected into tissue is compared with the mass delivered to a confined local target, not scaled by body weight.

The GHK-Cu dose of 0.5–1 mg is based on [Maquart, 1993], which found dose-dependent increases in collagen, total protein, and glycosaminoglycan accumulation across that range in rat wound chambers. Daily dosing is based on [Canapp, 2003], where daily GHK-Cu treatment for 13 days accelerated healing of full-thickness ischemic wounds.

The short exposure time of GHK-Cu triggers a downstream signal. Repeating the pulse daily probably works better (and stings less) than larger, infrequent doses.

Mice received up to 20 mg/kg GHK-Cu intraperitoneally every other day for 12 weeks, an HED of about 1.6 mg/kg, or 130 mg for an 80 kg adult, with therapeutic effects and no reported treatment-related toxicity [Zhang, 2022]. That’s two orders of magnitude larger.

Tβ4 blind local injection in humans is estimated around 200 to 250 mcg. The lower bound of 10 to 50 mcg is scaled to humans from Xu, where 1 mcg retained in fibrin improved rat ligament healing. But unlike fibrin-retained doses, blind aqueous injections spread and may miss difficult-to-target injuries, so they require a larger dose.

Increasing the dose also increases systemic exposure and the amount of Tβ4 available to stimulate angiogenesis and cell migration outside the injury, keeping the dose near the low end of what is likely to work with reduced systemic effects.

The rat Achilles study is not the anchor, because 60 mcg/kg/day was given intraperitoneally. Scaling it produces a systemic dose, which is the route the article argues against. Once-daily dosing matches both the ligament study and the human trial, and nothing supports splitting it.

Why not GLOW?

GLOW blends 50 mg GHK-Cu, 10 mg BPC-157, and 10 mg TB-500, using the shorter TB-500 fragment instead of full-length Tβ4. The fixed ratio provides less than half the BPC-157 indicated and does not accommodate twice-daily BPC dosing without also redosing the other two. It also stores GHK-Cu with the other peptides for weeks, which may allow copper-mediated oxidation over time. Separate vials avoid all three problems.

Peptides are signals, not substrates

These peptides provide repair signals. GHK-Cu also supplies copper, an essential cofactor for lysyl oxidase and collagen cross-linking. Repair also requires the substrates and cofactors needed to build connective tissue, including amino acids found in collagen peptides and vitamin C. Hyaluronic acid contributes to the extracellular matrix and repair process.

For connective-tissue repair, 15 g of gelatin or collagen peptides with about 50 mg of vitamin C 30–60 minutes before exercise and loading supplies collagen-specific amino acids when synthesis is being stimulated [Shaw, 2017]. Oral hyaluronic acid at about 100–200 mg/day can support connective-tissue hydration and viscoelasticity during repair [Oliva, 2021].

What local delivery does and does not establish

To date, no study has measured local versus systemic tissue exposure in the same human musculoskeletal injury, but the available evidence favors site injection to maximize exposure at the injury while minimizing total dose and systemic exposure.
