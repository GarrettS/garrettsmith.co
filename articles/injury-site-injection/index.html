DIY Injury Repair — Injecting BPC-157, TB-500, and GHK-Cu Locally

2026 — All Articles

For informational purposes only. For medical advice or diagnosis, consult a healthcare professional.

In rodents, BPC-157, Tβ4, and GHK-Cu improve healing. Subcutaneous injection is systemic, so the reasoning goes that that should work in humans, and many report that it does.

This article argues for injecting at the injury itself, to carefully put more of the dose where it is needed, for better expected healing and less systemic spillover.

It also covers what to buy, what to look for on the Certificate of Analysis (COA), and how to build a recovery program around other peptides, nutrition, and exercise.

Injecting 🔗

Local injection through a fine, sharp, sterile needle delivers the dose to the injury with minimal scarring. Use a thick needle to draw and 32 gauge or finer to inject.

Deeper injuries need a longer and possibly thicker needle, 30-31g — a bigger hole, more scarring, fewer passes.

For tendon problems, injecting near either end (its adjoining muscle or bony attachment) reaches its blood supply but clears fast. Injecting near the tendon’s midpoint drains slowly, but can be hard to hit blind (unguided), and risks touching (and scarring) the tendon. Both should still beat a systemic subcutaneous dose.

Concentrated GHK-Cu stings. A more dilute reconstitution reduces the sting.

Every injection risks infection, bleeding, and nerve or vessel damage. Always use sterile technique — wash your hands, wipe the vial stopper and the skin with alcohol, and use a fresh sterile needle each time. Pain that builds over the following hours or days, or spreading redness, swelling, warmth, or fever, are signs of infection that needs medical care.

A Closer Look at Human Subcutaneous Injection 🔗

Most of the subcutaneous dose leaves the site to enter circulation, dispersing through the organism, with a fraction reaching the injured tissue through its blood supply.

Humans are roughly two orders of magnitude heavier than rats, with liters rather than milliliters of blood and centimeter rather than millimeter delivery paths to sparsely vascularized tendons. The rats-therefore-humans inference doesn't hold.

Injecting SC over the injury does not change the delivery problem. Some of the dose is broken down locally, and the rest drains into capillaries and lymphatics and goes systemic, reaching the injury only as a small circulating fraction. If the injectate is below the SC fat, some might reach the deep fascia and the muscle just beneath it.

Local injection starts in the target tissue, before systemic dilution. How much that advantage matters depends on the peptide.

BPC-157 🔗

How It Works

BPC-157 upregulates growth hormone receptor (GHR) [Chang, 2014] and vascular endothelial growth factor receptor (VEGFR2) [Hsieh, 2017], and healing occurs for days following clearance [Mateescu, 2026].

GHR activation drives collagen production. Activated VEGFR2 leads to new vessel formation. The new vessels bring in nutrients, growth factors, and macrophages, and remove waste.

BPC-157 facilitates the repair that makes inflammation go away [Vasireddi, 2025], [Yıldırım, 2026]. As the repair clears and rebuilds the tissue, the inflammation resolves with the damage that drove it. NSAIDs can interfere with that repair signaling [Cohen, 2006].

Why Local

Systemic doses depend on blood carrying the peptide to the target intact.

Rats’ short circulation paths and fast heart rate (about 300 beats/min) probably shorten BPC-157 delivery time, helping more of the dose reach injuries before clearance, given its roughly 15-minute plasma half-life. Unsurprisingly, research shows intraperitoneal BPC-157 works in rats for tendon [Staresinic, 2003] and tendon-to-bone healing [Krivic, 2006].

Intramuscular (IM) injection at the injury gets the full dose to the target instantly, with systemic spillover at 14–19% in rats and 45–51% in dogs [He, 2022].

How Long, and How Often?

Twice daily is a practical floor for BPC-157, not an optimum. Its half-life is 5–15 minutes in rats and beagles [He, 2022], possibly longer in humans, and the GHR upregulation behind its tendon effect builds with sustained exposure, still rising at day three in cultured rat tendon fibroblasts [Chang, 2014]. A single daily shot leaves the receptor unstimulated for most of the day, so if that pattern holds in tissue, frequent dosing approximates it better than one large dose.

After IM injection, blood BPC-157 was undetectable by four hours [He, 2022]. Its concentration and persistence at the injury remain unmeasured.

Blind injections can miss the target. Small injections might be effective in vitro, but decrease the odds of reaching it. More frequent injections can improve those odds and should also keep local GHRs upregulated. But injections damage and scar tissue, and larger doses increase systemic spillover, so more isn’t better.

Dosing can go with events that support repair. Training raises local perfusion, adds mechanical stimulus, and raises GH; hot showers increase perfusion (and cleanse the skin). GH also rises around sleep and acts through the GHR BPC-157 upregulates. Dosing just before training makes the peptide and mechanical signals coincide rather than follow each other.

Dose

The BPC-157 dose shown here is 400–1000 µg a day, split across 2–4 injections. This wide range is based on conventional dosing, animal pharmacokinetics, GHR turnover, injury type and severity, rather than measured healing outcomes in humans.

Cancer Risk

BPC-157 promotes angiogenesis [Hsieh, 2017], and upregulates GHR [Chang, 2014]. If it circulates beyond the injury, it could support hidden tumors.

GH Secretagogues 🔗

GH secretagogues like tesamorelin or GRF (1-29) benefit the upregulated GH receptors. This also raises systemic IGF-1, which is associated with higher cancer risk. These peptides act on the anterior pituitary and should not be locally injected.

Thymosin Beta-4 vs "TB-500" 🔗

TB-500 is a seven-amino-acid fragment, Ac-LKKTETQ, of a 43-amino-acid protein, thymosin beta-4 (Tβ4). What the evidence shows for Tβ4 cannot be assumed to hold for TB-500 [McGuire, 2026].

Tβ4 Systemic vs Local and Mechanism of Action

Tβ4 binds G-actin to drive cell migration to injured tissue, and it also promotes vessel formation and cell survival [Xing, 2021]. Animal studies, covered below, show it improves healing in connective tissue and skin.

Local administration works. [Xu, 2013] found that 1 µg of Tβ4 in fibrin, placed into a transected rat medial collateral ligament, improved collagen organization and biomechanics compared with fibrin alone.

Local delivery matched the result of a far larger systemic dose, with about 4% as much Tβ4 in total. In a full-thickness rat skin wound, [Malinda, 1999] applied 5 µg of Tβ4 locally just twice, against 60 µg injected intraperitoneally every other day for seven days. The local route produced at least the same healing — more collagen deposition and angiogenesis — with effects by day 4.

G-actin is the soluble actin cells assemble into filaments to change shape and move, so binding it drives the migration of endothelial cells that form new vessels. In muscle, injured fibers and nearby immune cells raise local Tβ4, and myoblasts, including those from satellite cells, move toward it, shown in mice and cell culture [Tokura, 2011].

Tβ4 may also limit scarring. The anti-scarring peptide Ac-SDKP is cut from one end of Tβ4 by two enzymes, meprin-α and prolyl oligopeptidase [Kumar, 2016]. The second turns up throughout the body, and meprin-α may arrive with the immune cells that flood an injury, though whether human immune cells carry it is disputed [Beckinger, 2026]. So full-length Tβ4 injected into injured tendon, ligament, or muscle might make Ac-SDKP there.

The Risk

The migration Tβ4 drives to heal also lets tumors invade and spread, and Tβ4 supports tumor growth in animal models [Xing, 2021]. Local injection limits that risk by keeping the dose low and most of it at the target, with less reaching the rest of the body.

TB-500, the Fragment

TB-500 cannot be assumed to do any of this. It is a small fragment, missing most of the protein, and it lacks the end Ac-SDKP is cut from, so it cannot make Ac-SDKP anywhere.

The one controlled test of the fragment found it inactive in vitro, tracing the wound-healing activity to a metabolite that appears only at low levels in rats [Rahaman, 2024].

[Biçer, 2026] is the only controlled tendon study of TB-500, giving rats 60 µg/kg/day intraperitoneally for four weeks after Achilles transection and Kessler repair. TB-500 raised maximum load to failure and improved histological scores versus controls, and adding BPC-157 gave no further benefit, possibly limited by the four-week window. But the paper identifies the product only as Peptide Sciences TB-500 (27-155-PS), and that supplier sold both the full protein and the fragment, so which one was tested is unknown.

Community reports make TB-500 worth investigating. No safety data of any kind, no demonstrated effect of the fragment, and the tumor risk carried by its parent yield a poor risk-reward ratio.

That poor ratio is why the blend holds the least TB-500, about 0.2 mg a day, or 1.4 mg a week, below the 2 to 5 mg a week common in community use. The common "Wolverine" blend of BPC-157 and TB-500 gives as much of this weakest, riskiest peptide as the well-supported one, the opposite of what the evidence supports.

GHK-Cu 🔗

GHK-Cu is a naturally occurring copper tripeptide of glycine (G), histidine (H), and lysine (K). It promotes wound healing, attraction of immune cells, antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects, collagen and GAG synthesis, blood vessel growth. GHK is the same tripeptide without the copper. Human plasma of 20-year-olds contains about 200 ng/mL GHK. By age 60, that level drops to ~80 ng/mL [Pickart & Margolina, 2018].

GHK-Cu signals repair and supplies a material used for it, copper. Copper is the cofactor lysyl oxidase uses to cross-link collagen and elastin into the load-bearing fibrils that give healed tissue its strength. Its signaling adds to that — pro-collagen, pro-angiogenic, and anti-inflammatory — shown in skin and wounds and relevant to repairing muscle, fascia, and tendon [Pickart & Margolina, 2018].

Delivery of GHK-Cu is key, and most of the research is local, rather than systemic. GHK-Cu binds to blood proteins, so a systemic dose is diluted and bound as it circulates, possibly some of it reaching the injury. Effects scale with concentration, and local injection puts it at the site, uncomplicated by perfusion and binding proteins.

Unlike TB-500, the evidence for GHK-Cu is strong, and matches the known mechanism. Applied to rat wound chambers, GHK-Cu raised collagen, total protein, DNA, and glycosaminoglycans in proportion to concentration [Maquart, 1993], and injected into the joint after ACL reconstruction it improved graft stiffness and knee laxity at six weeks, though the gain was gone by twelve [Fu, 2015]. Systemic evidence for GHK-Cu is thinner, but weekly intraperitoneal GHK-Cu at 2 mg/kg preserved muscle mass, fiber size, and grip strength in mice with cigarette-smoke-induced wasting [Deng, 2023].

Dose

The GHK-Cu dose shown here is about 0.5–1 mg a day. [Maquart, 1993] found a dose-dependent increase in collagen accumulation with 0.5–2 mg GHK-Cu per injection into rat wound chambers, given every three days. Daily topical GHK-Cu also accelerated healing of ischemic skin wounds in rats [Canapp, 2003].

GHK-Cu is about 16% copper by mass, so 1 mg carries roughly 160 µg of copper, under a fifth of the recommended dietary intake. [Zhang, 2022] gave mice a human-equivalent near 130 mg for emphysema, over a hundred times the amount shown here, though that study did not assess toxicity.

Checking the COA 🔗

Products sold for research use are not made for human injection, and some have been reported as contaminated — an independent test of ten vials, BPC-157 and GHK-Cu among them, found several with low purity, endotoxin, and heavy metals. Ask for a certificate of analysis covering identity, purity, and endotoxin before buying.

The Certificate of Analysis (COA) tells the mass of the peptide. TB-500 fragment is ~889 Da, Tβ4 ~4963 Da.

COA Checklist

  • The test comes from an independent lab, not the seller.
  • The report can be looked up on the testing lab's own website and shows the same result, so an edited PDF from the seller will not match.
  • The lot number on the COA matches the lot on your vial, because a certificate describes only the batch it was run on.
  • The report covers what matters for an injection — identity, purity, residual TFA, and endotoxin.

ISO/IEC 17025:2017 accreditation is a plus, not a requirement, and most labs here do not carry it, Janoshik and Freedom Diagnostics included. It means an accreditor — A2LA or PJLA in the United States — audited the lab and lists it on a public site you can search.

A careful supplier runs chemistry at an ISO/IEC 17025 accredited lab and tests endotoxin. The most careful suppliers spread the tests across more than one accredited lab, a second re-running the chemistry. Vanguard Laboratory and Eagle Analytical carry that accreditation through A2LA, Ethos Analytics through PJLA.

These tests cost money, and high TFA or a bad mass check does not help sales, so suppliers often leave them off. Ask the supplier for both.

Please provide batch-specific COAs for:

  1. Counterion / Trifluoroacetic Acid (TFA) content analysis.
  2. Electrospray Ionization Mass Spectrometry (ESI-MS) showing observed molecular mass (deconvoluted Da) for:
    • GHK-Cu: To verify chelated copper complex mass (~402 Da) and peptide identity.
    • TB-500: To confirm the peptide's molecular mass.
    • Tesamorelin & BPC-157: To confirm intact parent molecular mass.

Residual TFA

Peptides purified with TFA are TFA salts, and standard research peptides can retain 10%–30%+ TFA by weight. That TFA counts in the labeled weight, so less of the vial is actual peptide. It also shortens the shelf life of the reconstituted solution, and it damages tissue at the injection site. Source from suppliers whose third-party COA reports the net peptide content and residual TFA under 0.5%.

Residual TFA in untested peptides can be 30% or more, causing pain and damaging tissue. Untested peptides are best avoided. But if they are injected, SC administration damages fat, which is less consequential than damaging the injured tissue.

Dosing and Local Injection Protocol Summary 🔗

This experimental blend combines all three peptides in 2–4 daily injections to renew BPC-157 exposure as it clears [He, 2022]. The blend’s efficacy, safety, and dosing are unestablished in humans.

Making the Blend

The 5:4:1 blend is prepared in two 10 mL vials. It uses half of the GHK-Cu and TB-500 source vials, leaving the remainder for another batch.

Each 10 mL vial gets 3 mL of peptide solutions:—
  • 0.5 mL GHK-Cu (12.5 mg),
  • 2 mL BPC-157 (10 mg),
  • and 0.5 mL TB-500 (2.5 mg)
—brought to a final volume of 10 mL with bacteriostatic water.

Materials

  • About 22 mL of bacteriostatic water (e.g., BAC Water Depot; not an affiliate link)
  • A 50 mg vial of GHK-Cu
  • Two 10 mg vials of BPC-157
  • A 10 mg vial of TB-500
  • Two sterile empty 10 mL vials
  • A 3 mL syringe for mixing
  • Alcohol swabs

Preparation

Sterile technique: a clean preparation area, disinfected vial stoppers, and sterile needles, syringes, and receiving vials.

  1. Reconstitute each peptide vial in 2 mL of water, swirling to dissolve (about a minute).
  2. Add 0.5 mL of GHK-Cu solution (12.5 mg) to the first blend vial.
  3. Add all 2 mL from one BPC-157 vial (10 mg).
  4. Add 0.5 mL of TB-500 solution (2.5 mg).
  5. Rinse the emptied BPC-157 vial with a little bacteriostatic water and transfer the rinse into the same blend vial.
  6. Add bacteriostatic water to bring the total volume to 10 mL, including the peptide solutions and rinse water.
  7. Repeat for the second blend vial, using the other BPC-157 vial.

Each vial holds 12.5 mg GHK-Cu, 10 mg BPC-157, and 2.5 mg TB-500 — the 5:4:1 blend at 1.25, 1.0, and 0.25 mg/mL. Refrigerate both, along with the leftover GHK-Cu and TB-500 for the next batch, and finish one before opening the second.

Dosing

GHK-Cu BPC-157 TB-500
In the blend (20 mL) 25 mg 20 mg 5 mg
Concentration 1.25 mg/mL 1.0 mg/mL 0.25 mg/mL
Per day 1.0 mg 0.80 mg 0.20 mg
Daily draw 80 units (0.80 mL), split into 2–4 injections of 20–40 units
Basis Rat wound studies [Maquart], [Canapp] GHR turnover + fast clearance Guess — no fragment dose data

Each injection is 20–40 units, so a one-unit slip is inconsequential. The 40 unit injection carries 0.5 mg GHK-Cu, which can sting and may need further dilution.

At 80 units a day, the two vial batch lasts about 25 days. The length follows from the batch size rather than from any measured outcome in humans for this blend. It matches the four weeks Biçer gave rats after Achilles repair [Biçer, 2026], though rats heal much faster.

Four injections a day over 25 days in a small area accrue scar tissue. A fresh needle each time, used only to inject and never to draw, keeps each puncture small.

Why Not GLOW?

GLOW typically blends 50 mg GHK-Cu, 10 mg BPC-157, and 10 mg TB-500, using the shorter TB-500 fragment. The fixed ratio is way off — BPC-157 at 25% and TB-500 at 100%.

Peptides Are Signals, Not Substrates 🔗

These peptides provide repair signals. The one exception is GHK-Cu, which also supplies copper, an essential cofactor for lysyl oxidase and collagen cross-linking.

High-quality complete protein and vitamin C are essential; humans can't synthesize them. Protein should be spread evenly across 3–4 meals a day, roughly 25–50 g each, 3–5 hours apart. Collagen peptides and gelatin might help and are low-risk.

Supplement Amount Timing
Complete protein ~1 g/lb lean mass 3–4×/day
Gelatin or collagen peptides 15 g Bedtime
Its ~3 g of glycine is the free-glycine dose shown to improve sleep [Yamadera, 2007].
Vitamin C 200 mg 1–2×/day

Collagen peptides might do two things an equal dose of complete protein cannot match.

First, they might signal fibroblasts to build extracellular matrix (ECM), the collagen and hyaluronic acid surrounding cells. A fraction crosses into the blood intact, as the dipeptide Pro-Hyp [Iwai, 2005], which in culture raises fibroblast proliferation and hyaluronic-acid synthesis [Ohara, 2010]. That rise cannot be substrate — hyaluronic acid is built from sugars, not amino acids — so Pro-Hyp is acting as a signal, and that peptide comes almost entirely from collagen.

Second, collagen supplies glycine, about a third of its amino acids, where whey and most proteins contain only a few percent. The body's glycine supply is limited, lower in older adults [Sekhar, 2011], and can fall below total demand, including the demand for collagen production [Meléndez-Hevia, 2009].

However, both mechanisms remain unproven in vivo. Compared with whey in older women, collagen gave no advantage for muscle connective-tissue synthesis [Oikawa, 2020], and in young men and women neither collagen nor whey raised it above no protein at all [Aussieker, 2023]. Trials in tendon, cartilage, and bone compare collagen only with non-protein placebo, which cannot separate a collagen effect from the extra protein.

What Local Delivery Does and Does Not Establish 🔗

To date, the human dose-response curve for BPC administration is unmeasured, and no study has measured local versus systemic tissue exposure in musculoskeletal injury. Site injection puts more of each dose at the injury and less everywhere else.
